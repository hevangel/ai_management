# Management Publication Writing & Cover Image Style Guide

## Purpose

This project is an editorial-style experiment.

The goal is to take the same underlying management idea, conversation, note, essay, or article and express it through five recognizably different management-publication styles:

1. MIT Sloan Management Review
2. Harvard Business Review
3. McKinsey & Company
4. Boston Consulting Group
5. Bain & Company

The objective is not to reproduce these organizations' exact editorial processes, proprietary research, branding, or visual identity.

The objective is to reproduce their recognizable **editorial logic**:

- what question they tend to ask
- what part of an idea they emphasize
- how they structure an argument
- what kind of framework they build
- what kind of conclusion they favor
- what kind of cover-image concept fits that treatment

The same source material should therefore produce **five genuinely different articles**, not five paraphrases.

---

# 1. Core Editorial Principle

The five styles should answer five different implicit questions.

| Style | Core question |
|---|---|
| **MIT Sloan Management Review** | What changed in the system, and why? |
| **Harvard Business Review** | What does this mean for managers, and what should they do differently? |
| **McKinsey** | What does this mean at enterprise scale, and how should the organization transform? |
| **BCG** | What new strategic logic is emerging, and where does competitive advantage move? |
| **Bain** | Where is value created or lost, and what actually works in execution? |

A successful transformation changes:

- thesis emphasis
- article structure
- vocabulary
- conceptual framing
- type of framework
- examples
- conclusion
- cover-image metaphor

It should not simply change tone.

---

# 2. Evidence Policy

## Style may be simulated. Evidence must not be fabricated.

This is the most important rule in the entire guide.

The system may freely generate:

- conceptual frameworks
- management terminology
- strategic lenses
- archetypes
- maturity models
- hypothetical scenarios
- analogies
- thought experiments
- visual metaphors
- managerial recommendations
- speculative future implications
- possible organizational models

The system must not fabricate:

- surveys
- percentages
- proprietary research
- executive interviews
- experiments
- datasets
- benchmark numbers
- academic citations
- company case studies presented as fact
- claims such as "our research shows"
- claims such as "we surveyed 500 executives"
- claims such as "leading companies are 37% more likely to..."

unless those facts are actually present in the source material or have been independently researched and verified.

## Conceptual writing is acceptable

An article does not need fake statistics to sound intelligent.

If no empirical evidence exists, the article should remain clearly:

- conceptual
- analytical
- experiential
- speculative
- practitioner-driven

A polished framework is acceptable.

Fake empirical authority is not.

## Quantification rule

Quantify only when real numbers exist.

Otherwise use:

- qualitative segmentation
- scenarios
- archetypes
- directional economics
- relative comparisons
- before/after models
- causal logic

Never manufacture numbers merely because consulting-style writing often contains charts.

---

# 3. Source Material Handling

Before writing any version, extract the following from the source:

## Central thesis

What is the main idea?

## Supporting observations

What has the author noticed, experienced, or inferred?

## Mechanism

Why does the author believe the effect occurs?

## Evidence

What facts, research, examples, or observations actually support the argument?

## Original insight

What is distinctive about the author's interpretation?

## Implications

What follows if the argument is correct?

## Uncertainty

Which parts are established, and which parts are predictions or speculation?

Do not add invented support merely to make the article sound more authoritative.

---

# 4. The Five Editorial Lenses

Before drafting, reinterpret the source through five different lenses.

## Sloan

**Find the underlying system.**

Ask:

- Which constraint changed?
- Which resource became abundant or scarce?
- How do incentives change?
- How does coordination change?
- How does information move differently?
- What second-order organizational effects follow?

## HBR

**Find the manager's dilemma.**

Ask:

- What familiar management practice stops working?
- What is the manager likely to misunderstand?
- What contradiction will the manager experience?
- What practical behavior should change?

## McKinsey

**Find the enterprise transformation.**

Ask:

- What does this mean at scale?
- Which operating-model components must change?
- What capabilities are needed?
- What stages might organizations move through?
- What would the executive team need to redesign?

## BCG

**Find the new source of advantage.**

Ask:

- What new scarcity emerges?
- What conventional wisdom becomes wrong?
- Where does differentiation move?
- What strategic paradox appears?
- What new conceptual lens explains the change?

## Bain

**Find the value equation.**

Ask:

- Where is value actually created?
- Where does value leak?
- What bottleneck prevents local gains from becoming business outcomes?
- What practical operating discipline fixes the problem?
- What would successful execution look like?

Only after these five angles are clear should the drafting begin.

# 5. MIT Sloan Management Review Style

## Editorial identity

Sloan-style writing sits between management research and executive practice.

It is particularly well suited to:

- technology
- organizational systems
- operating models
- incentives
- coordination
- information flows
- knowledge
- management architecture
- second-order consequences of technology

The ideal article feels like:

**A technically sophisticated practitioner explaining why an organizational system behaves differently after an important constraint changes.**

The reader should finish thinking:

> I understand the system differently now.

---

## Intellectual orientation

Favor:

- causal reasoning
- organizational mechanisms
- incentives
- systems
- coordination
- workflow
- information architecture
- knowledge distribution
- decision structures
- organizational design
- unintended consequences

The article should explain **why** an effect happens.

A strong Sloan-style reasoning chain often looks like:

**Technology changes a constraint  
→ behavior changes  
→ bottlenecks move  
→ organizational structure adapts  
→ management practices must change**

---

## Tone

Use a tone that is:

- analytical
- calm
- intellectually serious
- technically comfortable
- skeptical of simplistic claims
- slightly academic without becoming unreadable

Avoid exaggerated rhetoric.

Prefer:

> AI changes the economics of execution.

Over:

> AI will completely reinvent everything we know about work.

---

## Typical structure

### 1. Describe the phenomenon

Explain what changed.

### 2. Identify the old assumption

What organizational assumption previously made sense?

### 3. Explain the mechanism

Why is that assumption becoming weaker?

### 4. Trace second-order effects

What other parts of the organization change?

### 5. Introduce a framework if useful

Frameworks should help explain the mechanism rather than decorate the article.

### 6. Discuss implications

What type of organization emerges?

### 7. End with the structural consequence

The conclusion should answer:

**If this mechanism continues, how will the organization itself change?**

---

## Sentence pattern

A common pattern is:

**Observation → mechanism → implication**

Example:

AI coding agents lower the marginal cost of producing software artifacts. That does not automatically lower the cost of producing reliable software because specification, review, architectural judgment, and integration remain scarce. The bottleneck therefore moves from code production toward verification and coordination.

---

## Evidence use

Use research when available.

When research is unavailable, rely on:

- explicit causal reasoning
- clearly marked practitioner observation
- hypothetical examples
- conceptual models

Do not invent studies or citations to simulate academic authority.

---

## Avoid

Do not turn Sloan into:

- generic leadership advice
- a consulting deck converted into prose
- a technology news story
- an AI hype essay
- an academic paper with unnecessary formalism

---

# 6. Sloan Cover Image Style

## Visual objective

Represent the **system or mechanism**.

The cover should show how the underlying organization works differently.

## Good visual themes

- networks
- information flows
- decision layers
- organizational structures
- humans interacting with systems
- modular architecture
- bottlenecks
- changing boundaries
- layered systems
- transformation from one structure into another

## Composition

Prefer:

- clean geometry
- intelligent editorial abstraction
- restrained complexity
- moderate technical detail
- generous negative space

The image may resemble a conceptual diagram without becoming a literal business diagram.

## Avoid

- humanoid robots
- glowing brains
- neon cyberpunk imagery
- executives staring at holograms
- generic circuit patterns
- stock-office imagery

## Sloan cover prompt skeleton

Create a sophisticated editorial illustration for a management-and-technology article about **[IDEA]**.

Visualize the underlying organizational mechanism rather than illustrating the topic literally.

Use an intelligent systems-oriented metaphor involving **[SYSTEM ELEMENTS]**.

The composition should feel analytical, technological, restrained, and appropriate for an executive publication concerned with management systems and organizational change.

Use clean geometry, subtle structural detail, generous negative space, and a modern editorial aesthetic.

Do not include publication logos, mastheads, generic robots, glowing brains, or clichéd corporate stock imagery.

# 7. Harvard Business Review Style

## Editorial identity

HBR-style writing begins with a **managerial problem**.

Where Sloan asks how the system works, HBR asks:

**What should a manager understand or do differently?**

The strongest HBR-style articles often contain a contradiction:

- the intuitive response is wrong
- an old management practice no longer works
- a common metric becomes misleading
- a leadership habit produces the opposite result
- a new technology changes what good management looks like

The reader should finish thinking:

> I need to change how I manage this.

---

## Intellectual orientation

Favor:

- leadership
- managerial judgment
- team behavior
- decision making
- talent
- incentives
- performance management
- practical strategy
- organizational behavior

There should usually be a recognizable human protagonist:

- manager
- team leader
- executive
- engineer
- product leader
- HR leader

---

## Tone

Use:

- authoritative but accessible prose
- memorable contrasts
- practical language
- relatively little jargon
- clear narrative flow
- actionable insight

HBR should sound intelligent without sounding academic.

---

## Typical structure

### 1. Begin with a recognizable management situation

Example:

A manager notices that one engineer suddenly produces three times as much code after adopting AI.

The obvious conclusion is improved performance.

That conclusion may be wrong.

### 2. Introduce the tension

Explain why conventional management logic fails.

### 3. State the central insight

Make the idea memorable.

Example:

**When production becomes cheap, judgment becomes valuable.**

### 4. Build a compact framework

Three to five components usually work well.

Example:

Managers should evaluate:

- problem selection
- judgment
- risk reduction
- organizational leverage

### 5. Show practical situations

Use examples to make the argument concrete.

### 6. Give managerial actions

Translate the idea into behavior.

### 7. Return to the central tension

End with a memorable managerial implication.

---

## Writing characteristics

Use strong contrasts:

- output vs. impact
- execution vs. judgment
- speed vs. learning
- automation vs. accountability
- activity vs. contribution

Example:

The question is no longer whether an engineer can produce code quickly. AI can make code production faster for almost everyone. The more important question is whether the engineer knows what should be built, what should not be built, and where failure would matter.

---

## Avoid

Do not make HBR sound like:

- an academic paper
- a consulting report
- an industry survey
- a software implementation manual
- a listicle with no real thesis

---

# 8. HBR Cover Image Style

## Visual objective

Represent **one memorable management tension**.

The image should communicate the article's central idea within seconds.

## Good visual themes

- balance
- comparison
- mismatch
- transformation
- a familiar object behaving unexpectedly
- person confronting a paradox
- two apparently identical outputs with different value
- bottleneck versus abundance
- choice under uncertainty

## Composition

Prefer:

- one dominant idea
- minimal clutter
- strong negative space
- conceptual illustration
- visual metaphor rather than literal explanation

## Example

Article:

**When AI Makes Output a Bad Performance Metric**

Possible visual:

A manager holding a tiny scale while an enormous pile of identical documents overwhelms the desk.

The image communicates:

**More output does not make evaluation easier.**

## HBR cover prompt skeleton

Create a premium editorial illustration for a management article about **[IDEA]**.

Express the central managerial tension through one simple and memorable visual metaphor: **[METAPHOR]**.

The image should be conceptually clever, immediately understandable, elegant, minimal, and appropriate for senior managers.

Use one dominant subject, clean composition, strong negative space, and polished editorial-art direction.

Do not include publication logos, mastheads, generic robots, glowing brains, text-heavy diagrams, or clichéd corporate imagery.

---

# 9. McKinsey Style

## Editorial identity

McKinsey-style writing treats the issue as an **enterprise transformation problem**.

The article should feel like:

**The prose version of an executive strategy deck.**

The reader should finish thinking:

> I can take this argument into an executive meeting.

---

## Intellectual orientation

Favor:

- enterprise transformation
- operating models
- capability building
- organizational design
- economics
- productivity
- maturity
- governance
- scaling
- implementation
- executive decisions

Translate technology into organizational consequences.

Instead of:

> AI writes software.

Prefer:

> AI changes the economics of software production and shifts the bottleneck toward specification, validation, integration, and accountability.

---

## Tone

Use:

- concise executive prose
- answer-first writing
- highly structured logic
- confident but qualified language
- parallel structure
- business terminology
- strong section architecture

McKinsey should often feel like a slide deck converted into an article.

---

## Typical structure

### 1. State the answer early

Do not spend half the article discovering the thesis.

Example:

AI agents may improve developer productivity, but companies that simply attach agents to existing workflows are likely to capture only part of the opportunity. The larger gains will come from redesigning the operating model around machine execution.

### 2. Define the transformation

Break the change into dimensions.

For example:

- work
- roles
- technology
- governance
- metrics
- organization

### 3. Segment organizations

Possible archetypes:

**AI-assisted organization**

AI is layered onto existing workflows.

**AI-native organization**

Workflows are redesigned around machine execution.

### 4. Describe the target operating model

Explain how the future organization differs.

### 5. Create a transformation path

Example:

Foundation  
→ workflow redesign  
→ orchestration  
→ operating-model redesign

### 6. End with executive choices

What decisions must leadership make?

---

## Quantification rule

Quantify only when real numbers are available.

Otherwise use:

- qualitative segmentation
- maturity stages
- archetypes
- directional economics
- capability gaps
- before/after comparisons

Never invent survey statistics.

---

## Exhibit thinking

Even without creating actual charts, structure arguments so they could become exhibits.

Possible exhibits:

- maturity model
- transformation roadmap
- before/after workflow
- operating-model architecture
- value chain
- capability map
- organizational archetypes
- bottleneck shift
- responsibility matrix

---

## Writing pattern

Use answer-first paragraphs.

First sentence:

**Conclusion.**

Remaining sentences:

**Reasoning and implication.**

Example:

Companies will need to redesign how work enters the system, how machines execute it, how humans validate it, and how accountability is assigned. The organizations that merely deploy AI tools without changing these surrounding systems may increase activity without materially improving end-to-end performance.

---

## Avoid

Avoid:

- long personal anecdotes
- philosophical digressions
- excessive humor
- speculative futurism without structure
- fake survey language
- invented "leader vs. laggard" statistics

---

# 10. McKinsey Cover Image Style

## Visual objective

Represent **enterprise transformation architecture**.

The image should suggest:

- scale
- structure
- operating-model change
- modularity
- transformation

## Visual vocabulary

Favor:

- systems
- networks
- geometry
- modular components
- pathways
- transformation structures
- operating-model abstraction
- organizational architecture

## Composition

Prefer:

- disciplined geometry
- structured abstraction
- minimal visual noise
- controlled complexity
- professional corporate editorial design

## Avoid

- whimsical cartoons
- dramatic science fiction
- playful character scenes
- generic business photography
- emotional narrative imagery

## McKinsey cover prompt skeleton

Create a high-end executive editorial image for an article about **[TRANSFORMATION]**.

Represent the topic as a structured enterprise transformation rather than a literal scene.

Use **[GEOMETRIC OR SYSTEM METAPHOR]** to suggest changes in operating model, organizational architecture, capability, and scale.

The design should feel disciplined, analytical, architectural, minimal, and suitable for senior executives.

Use precise geometry, controlled complexity, generous negative space, and sophisticated corporate editorial design.

No logos, mastheads, text labels, generic office photography, robots, or science-fiction imagery.

# 11. BCG Style

## Editorial identity

BCG-style writing focuses on the **new strategic logic** created by a change.

It is generally more:

- conceptual
- future-oriented
- provocative
- strategy-driven

than McKinsey.

The reader should finish thinking:

> This gives me a new lens for understanding competitive advantage.

---

## Intellectual orientation

Favor:

- competitive advantage
- strategic differentiation
- new scarcity
- emerging economics
- future business models
- technology-driven strategy
- organizational adaptation
- strategic paradox
- capability advantage

A strong BCG-style article often begins with a contradiction.

Example:

AI makes execution cheaper.

That may make good management more valuable, not less.

---

## Tone

Use:

- strategic
- polished
- intellectually adventurous
- future-facing
- provocative but not sensational
- concept-driven

BCG can support stronger conceptual language than McKinsey.

---

## Typical structure

### 1. Introduce the paradox

Conventional expectation:

AI reduces the importance of human management.

Counterargument:

As execution becomes abundant, judgment becomes more important.

### 2. Name the phenomenon

A useful term may help compress the idea.

Examples:

- Judgment Economy
- Verification Bottleneck
- Agent Leverage
- Intelligence Abundance
- Organizational Intelligence Density

Do not invent terminology merely for branding.

The term must clarify something.

### 3. Explain the mechanism

Why does the strategic landscape change?

### 4. Identify the new source of advantage

Possible advantages:

- proprietary context
- better organizational learning
- workflow design
- institutional judgment
- orchestration
- governance
- speed of adaptation

### 5. Identify strategic choices

What tradeoffs must firms make?

### 6. Describe the future state

What might the winning organization look like?

---

## Writing characteristics

Use memorable synthesis.

Example:

When intelligence becomes abundant, competitive advantage shifts from owning intelligence to organizing it.

Another example:

AI may commoditize execution while increasing the premium on institutional judgment.

---

## Evidence rule

The strategic concept may be speculative.

Make that explicit.

Do not present a new framework as scientifically established merely because it sounds elegant.

Use language such as:

- may
- suggests
- could
- appears to
- our argument is
- one possible implication is

when evidence is incomplete.

---

## Avoid

- unnecessary jargon
- fake proprietary research
- invented percentages
- frameworks that do not clarify the problem
- excessive implementation detail

---

# 12. BCG Cover Image Style

## Visual objective

Represent the **new strategic concept**.

The cover may be more visually adventurous than McKinsey.

## Good themes

- emergence
- transformation
- tension
- competition
- scale
- abundance versus scarcity
- ecosystems
- competing structures
- strategic landscapes
- unexpected combinations of familiar objects

## Composition

Prefer:

- bold conceptual center
- elegant abstraction
- strong graphic structure
- greater visual energy than McKinsey
- intellectually interesting tension

## Example

Article:

**The Judgment Economy**

Possible image:

Thousands of identical mechanical hands feeding into one small human decision point.

The concept is immediately visible:

**Execution is abundant. Judgment is scarce.**

## BCG cover prompt skeleton

Create a bold conceptual editorial image for a strategy article about **[STRATEGIC IDEA]**.

Visualize the emerging strategic logic through an original metaphor involving **[METAPHOR]**.

The artwork should feel intelligent, future-facing, concept-driven, sophisticated, and slightly provocative.

It should suggest a new way of thinking about competitive advantage rather than literally illustrating a business process.

Use strong graphic composition, elegant abstraction, meaningful geometry, visual tension, and generous negative space.

No logos, mastheads, publication names, text-heavy diagrams, humanoid robots, glowing brains, or clichéd technology imagery.

# 13. Bain Style

## Editorial identity

Bain-style writing focuses on **value creation and execution**.

It is the most operator-oriented of the five.

The central question is:

**What actually produces business results?**

The reader should finish thinking:

> I know what I would change operationally.

---

## Intellectual orientation

Favor:

- value creation
- business economics
- execution
- accountability
- customer outcomes
- operational discipline
- repeatability
- organizational simplicity
- performance
- measurable outcomes

Bain should move closer than the others to:

**What should the management team actually do next?**

---

## Tone

Use:

- direct
- pragmatic
- commercially grounded
- confident
- concise
- action-oriented

Avoid unnecessary theoretical exposition.

---

## Typical structure

### 1. Start with the value problem

Example:

Many organizations can demonstrate that AI helps developers produce code faster. Far fewer can show that these gains reduce product cycle time, improve reliability, or lower total development cost.

That is the actual management problem.

### 2. Identify where value leaks

Example:

AI increases code generation.

But:

- review expands
- integration remains slow
- rework increases
- priorities remain poor
- downstream teams remain constrained

Therefore local productivity does not necessarily become enterprise value.

### 3. Define the winning model

What practical disciplines preserve the gain?

### 4. Give an operational framework

Example:

- choose the right work
- redesign the workflow
- control verification cost
- measure outcomes
- scale what works

### 5. Connect to economics

Where appropriate, discuss:

- cost
- speed
- quality
- customer outcomes
- revenue
- productivity
- capital efficiency

### 6. End with the result

Technology adoption is not the outcome.

Business performance is.

---

## Writing characteristics

Use direct contrasts.

Example:

Generating more code is easy to measure.

Generating more value is harder.

Another example:

A productivity gain that creates an equal increase in review cost is not a productivity gain at the enterprise level.

---

## Avoid

Do not turn Bain into:

- a research journal
- a futurist manifesto
- abstract organizational theory
- a complex conceptual framework
- fake benchmark research

---

## Visual objective

The image should communicate:

## Good themes

- narrow bottlenecks
- machines
- conversion
- pathways

- acceleration

## Composition

Prefer:

- bold simple idea
- clear visual hierarchy
- directional movement
- practical rather than philosophical metaphor
- more energy than McKinsey

## Bain cover prompt skeleton

Create a sharp executive editorial image for an article about **[BUSINESS PROBLEM]**.

Represent how **[INPUT OR TECHNOLOGY]** is converted into **[BUSINESS OUTCOME]**, using a strong visual metaphor involving **[METAPHOR]**.

The image should communicate execution, leverage, performance, and measurable value.

Make it bold, simple, energetic, polished, and appropriate for senior business leaders.

Use clear visual hierarchy, directional movement, controlled geometry, and ample negative space.

No logos, mastheads, publication names, generic stock photography, humanoid robots, glowing brains, or decorative science-fiction imagery.

# 15. Same Idea, Five Different Articles

Assume the source idea is:

> AI agents can perform much of the execution work previously assigned to junior software engineers.

The five treatments should diverge.

## Sloan

### Question

How does abundant machine execution change organizational structure?

### Thesis

Organizations built around scarce human execution capacity may reorganize around judgment, verification, coordination, and accountability.

### Possible title

**When Execution Stops Being the Bottleneck**

---

## HBR

### Question

How should managers evaluate engineers when AI can perform much of the visible work?

### Thesis

Managers must stop equating production with contribution and place greater weight on judgment, problem selection, and impact.

### Possible title

**When AI Makes Output a Bad Performance Metric**

---

## McKinsey

### Question

How should the engineering operating model change?

### Thesis

Capturing the full benefit of AI agents requires redesigning roles, workflows, platforms, governance, and performance systems rather than simply deploying copilots.

### Possible title

**Rewiring Engineering for the Agentic Enterprise**

---

## BCG

### Question

Where does competitive advantage move?

### Thesis

As execution becomes more widely available, advantage shifts toward organizations that combine machine intelligence with context, judgment, orchestration, and institutional learning.

### Possible title

**Competing in the Judgment Economy**

---

## Bain

### Question

How do companies turn machine productivity into business value?

### Thesis

AI-generated output creates little value unless organizations remove downstream bottlenecks in review, integration, prioritization, and deployment.

### Possible title

**Why More AI-Generated Code Doesn't Necessarily Create More Value**

---

# 17. Universal Cover Image Rules

## Find the argument before designing the picture

Do not generate:

> AI article = robot at laptop.

First reduce the article to one conceptual sentence.

Then find a visual metaphor.

Example:

Article thesis:

> AI makes production abundant while verification remains scarce.

Visual metaphor:

> A giant pipeline feeding into a tiny inspection gate.

That is the cover.

---

## One image, one idea

Do not try to visualize the whole article.

Use the strongest conceptual insight.

---

## Avoid default AI imagery

Avoid unless genuinely required:

- robot heads
- robot hands
- glowing brains
- blue holograms
- neural-network faces
- circuit-board people
- human finger touching robot finger
- Matrix-style code
- executives looking at transparent screens

These communicate:

**technology**

but rarely communicate:

**the argument.**

---

## Minimize text

The artwork should work without explanatory text.

Titles and publication labels should generally be added separately.

---

## Use conceptual metaphors

Good editorial metaphors often involve:

- scale
- funnels
- bridges
- bottlenecks
- balance
- transformation
- networks
- layers
- gates
- pathways
- fragmentation
- convergence
- multiplication
- scarcity
- abundance

# 18. Generating All Five Covers Together

When generating a comparison board containing all five styles:

Use five distinct editorial treatments.

Do not create the same image five times with:

- different colors
- different crops
- different typography

Each panel should reinterpret the article through the corresponding editorial lens.

Recommended layout:

**Five equally sized panels in a horizontal strip or balanced grid.**

Labels may identify:

- Sloan
- HBR
- McKinsey
- BCG
- Bain

Do not reproduce official logos or mastheads.

The five panels should remain distinguishable even if the labels are removed.

# 19. Article Generation Workflow

## Step 1 — Extract the source idea

Identify:

- thesis
- observations
- mechanism
- evidence
- examples
- original insight
- uncertainties

---

## Step 2 — Separate fact from interpretation

Maintain three categories:

### Established

What is actually known or supported.

### Interpretation

What the author concludes.

### Speculation

What may happen in the future.

Do not blur them.

---

## Step 3 — Find five different angles

Write one sentence for each:

**Sloan:** the organizational mechanism is...

**HBR:** the managerial problem is...

**McKinsey:** the enterprise transformation is...

**BCG:** the new strategic advantage is...

**Bain:** the value-creation problem is...

If those five sentences sound nearly identical, the articles are not differentiated enough.

---

## Step 4 — Draft independently

Do not copy the structure of the original article five times.

Write each version as if it were independently commissioned.

---

## Step 5 — Create frameworks only when useful

Frameworks should compress reasoning.

They should not exist simply because management writing often contains boxes.

---

## Step 6 — Check for fake authority

Before finalizing, search the draft mentally for claims such as:

- studies show
- research proves
- leaders outperform
- companies are X% more likely
- executives report
- top quartile
- benchmark
- survey

If the source does not support those claims, remove them.

---

## Step 7 — Design the cover

Reduce each article to one conceptual sentence.

Then ask:

**What physical situation visually represents this argument?**

Generate the metaphor first.

Generate the artwork second.

---

3. McKinsey & Company
4. Boston Consulting Group
5. Bain & Company

Do not merely rewrite the same article using different vocabulary.

First identify:

- the original author's central thesis
- supporting observations
- mechanisms
- evidence
- examples
- uncertainties
- original insights

Preserve the author's substantive argument.

Do not invent empirical support.

### Evidence policy

You may generate:

- conceptual frameworks
- terminology
- strategic lenses
- archetypes
- maturity models
- hypothetical examples
- analogies
- managerial implications
- speculative future scenarios

You must not fabricate:

- surveys
- statistics
- interviews
- proprietary research
- benchmarks
- academic citations
- company case studies presented as factual
- claims such as "our research shows"

unless those facts are actually present in the source or have been independently researched and verified.

When no empirical evidence exists, keep the article conceptual, analytical, experiential, or speculative.

### Editorial treatments

**MIT Sloan Management Review**

Focus on the underlying organizational or technological mechanism.

Explain how changing constraints, incentives, information, technology, or coordination alter the management system.

Emphasize causal reasoning and second-order effects.

---

**Harvard Business Review**

Focus on the managerial dilemma.

Begin with a recognizable management problem.

Challenge conventional wisdom.

Develop a memorable central insight.

Provide a practical framework for managers.

---

**McKinsey**

Focus on enterprise transformation.

Use answer-first writing.

Explain implications for operating models, capabilities, governance, roles, and implementation.

Think in potential exhibits.

Quantify only when real data exists.

---

**BCG**

Focus on the emerging strategic logic.

Identify a paradox, new scarcity, or new source of competitive advantage.

Develop a strong conceptual lens.

Explain strategic choices and future implications.

---

**Bain**

Focus on value creation and execution.

Identify where economic value is created or lost.

Explain what prevents local productivity gains from becoming business results.

Provide a pragmatic operating approach.

---

The five articles must differ materially in:

- thesis emphasis
- structure
- vocabulary
- conceptual framework
- examples
- conclusion

# Cover Image Generation Style Guide

## Purpose

The cover image is not merely an illustration for the article.

It should look like a **finished management magazine cover, consulting insight cover, or executive report cover** that could plausibly appear on a publication website, PDF report, LinkedIn post, or editorial landing page.

The objective is to reproduce the recognizable **visual grammar** associated with:

1. MIT Sloan Management Review
2. Harvard Business Review
3. McKinsey & Company
4. Boston Consulting Group
5. Bain & Company

The five covers should feel materially different from one another.

Do not simply generate five conceptual illustrations in different colors.

Each cover should have its own:

- editorial hierarchy
- typography strategy
- page architecture
- visual metaphor
- use of diagrams versus illustration
- density of information
- palette
- relationship between text and image

The final result should feel like a designed publication cover, not like AI-generated concept art with a title pasted on top.

---

# 1. Finished-Cover Principle

Every generated cover should contain a recognizable publication structure.

At minimum:

### Publication identity area

Usually at the top.

May contain:

- publication name
- publication-style masthead
- section name
- small editorial tagline
- report-series label

### Main headline

The dominant text element.

It should be visually large enough that the cover still works as a thumbnail.

### Dek / subtitle

One short sentence explaining the management argument.

### Hero visual

This may be:

- editorial illustration
- conceptual photography
- infographic
- strategic diagram
- system architecture
- data-like graphic
- symbolic landscape
- visual metaphor

The hero visual should communicate the article's thesis.

### Supporting editorial details

Optional elements include:

- topic labels
- section markers
- small framework labels
- author name
- author descriptor
- issue/category metadata
- small icons
- short callouts

These details make the image feel like an actual publication cover rather than a poster.

---

# 2. Typography Is Part of the Image

Typography is not an afterthought.

The cover should visually communicate editorial authority before the reader understands the illustration.

Use a clear hierarchy:

**Publication masthead**

↓

**Main headline**

↓

**Dek**

↓

**Hero visual**

↓

**Small supporting information**

The headline should normally occupy a substantial part of the upper third or upper half of the page.

Do not shrink the headline merely to make more room for artwork.

Management publication covers are primarily **editorial design**, not artwork with captions.

---

# 3. Cover Aspect Ratio

Default to portrait publication proportions.

Preferred:

**2:3 portrait**

or approximately:

**4:5 portrait**

The layout should resemble:

- magazine cover
- report cover
- executive insight cover
- thought-leadership PDF cover

When generating all five together, use five equal portrait panels.

Do not generate five landscape illustrations and place publication labels above them.

---

# 4. Text Density

Use enough text to establish publication identity, but not so much that generation becomes unreliable.

Prioritize text in this order:

1. publication name
2. article title
3. article subtitle
4. author
5. framework labels
6. minor metadata

Avoid long paragraphs.

Short framework labels are acceptable.

Examples:

- INTENT
- GENERATE
- VERIFY
- LEARN
- HUMAN JUDGMENT
- BUSINESS VALUE
- AI EXECUTION
- BETTER OUTCOMES

Do not invent quantitative data merely because a diagram appears on the cover.

---

# 5. No Fake Empirical Authority

The evidence policy for the articles also applies to the covers.

The cover may contain conceptual diagrams.

It must not contain invented claims such as:

- 73% productivity improvement
- 4.6× higher returns
- surveyed 2,500 executives
- top-quartile companies
- our global benchmark

unless those numbers actually exist in the source material.

Conceptual labels are encouraged.

Fake data is not.

---

# 6. Two Major Cover Families

The five publications belong to two slightly different visual families.

## Management magazine

### MIT Sloan Management Review

### Harvard Business Review

These should feel like editorial magazine covers.

They may contain:

- strong editorial typography
- provocative illustration
- visual metaphor
- human narrative
- relatively expressive layout

---

## Consulting insight / report

### McKinsey

### BCG

### Bain

These should feel more like:

- executive reports
- consulting insights
- strategic research covers
- CEO briefing documents

They should generally have:

- stronger corporate identity
- more disciplined layout
- clearer framework visualization
- greater use of diagrams, architecture, systems, or strategic imagery

The consulting covers should not simply look like business magazines.

---

# 7. MIT Sloan Management Review Cover Style

## Core character

**Academic-management magazine meets technology and organizational systems.**

The cover should feel:

- intellectual
- analytical
- contemporary
- slightly academic
- technology-aware
- editorial rather than corporate

Think:

**research magazine + systems thinking + restrained illustration**

---

## Page architecture

Typical structure:

**Top left:** publication masthead

**Top/right corner:** small editorial tagline or topic labels

**Upper third:** large serif headline

**Below headline:** concise explanatory dek

**Lower half:** conceptual systems illustration or infographic

**Bottom:** topic taxonomy and/or author line

---

## Typography

Use:

- strong editorial serif for headline
- clean sans-serif for supporting text
- substantial contrast between title and metadata

The typography should feel like a serious university-affiliated management publication.

Headline examples:

> When Code Becomes Abundant

> A New Unit of Organizational Capacity

> Managing the Verification Bottleneck

---

## Color

Preferred visual character:

- warm white
- cream
- light gray
- restrained red accent
- muted blue
- technical cyan
- occasional orange accent

Avoid overly glossy corporate gradients.

---

## Hero visual

Prefer a visual that explains a **system**.

Examples:

- human capacity on one side, AI capacity on another
- flowing organizational network
- intent → generation → verification → learning loop
- execution volume shifting toward judgment
- organizational architecture being reconfigured
- multiple AI agents connected around a human decision layer

Illustration can be moderately diagrammatic.

---

## Sloan cover formula

**Editorial masthead + serious serif headline + restrained subtitle + systems illustration + small academic/editorial details**

---

## Sloan prompt template

"[TITLE]"

Subtitle:

"[SUBTITLE]"

[VISUAL METAPHOR]

Use restrained diagrammatic elements, organizational flows, human figures, technology components, arrows, layers, or networks where useful.

Color character: cream, black, muted blue, restrained red accents, optional orange or cyan highlights.

Add small editorial details such as topic categories, short framework labels, and an author line near the bottom.

The overall design should feel like a serious technology-and-management research magazine: analytical, intelligent, restrained, highly legible, and professionally art-directed.

Avoid generic robot imagery, glowing AI brains, neon cyberpunk aesthetics, stock-photo executives, or decorative technology effects.

# 16. Cover Quality Checklist

Before accepting a generated cover, check:

### Does it look like a publication?

Not merely a poster?

### Can the headline be read immediately?

If not, typography is too weak.

### Is there a clear masthead area?

The page should have publication identity.

### Is there a visual hierarchy?

Masthead → headline → subtitle → visual → metadata.

### Does the visual communicate the article's argument?

Not merely "AI" or "business."

### Is the style recognizably different from the other four?

Especially:

McKinsey ≠ BCG ≠ Bain.

### Is there fake data?

If yes, remove it.

### Is there too much decorative AI imagery?

If yes, simplify it.

### Would it look plausible as a LinkedIn thumbnail or PDF report cover?

If yes, the design is working.
