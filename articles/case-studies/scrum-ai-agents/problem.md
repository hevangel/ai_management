# Scrum in the Age of AI Agents: When Execution Becomes Abundant

## The business problem

For most of software engineering's history, execution capacity was scarce. Organizations employed developers because turning an idea into working software required large amounts of human labor, and the management systems built around Agile methods — estimation, story points, velocity, Sprint planning, utilization targets — all assumed that producing code was the bottleneck.

AI agents are inverting that assumption. An engineer who previously implemented one piece of work at a time can increasingly delegate coding, testing, documentation, investigation, and refactoring to multiple AI agents operating in parallel. When a five-person team can generate dozens of implementations, tests, and experiments simultaneously, the constraint on software development moves away from producing changes and toward deciding what changes should be made, determining whether they are correct, and understanding what has been learned after they are deployed.

This creates an uncomfortable management problem. The organizations have spent two decades building delivery systems around scarce human execution — yet execution is becoming abundant. Meanwhile DORA's research warns that AI acts as an **amplifier**: higher AI adoption is associated with higher delivery throughput *and* higher instability at the same time. More output is not more productivity if the organization cannot absorb what its agents produce.

The question for technology leaders is no longer "How do we produce software faster?" It is: **How much machine-generated change can the organization safely understand, validate, absorb, and learn from — and what operating model makes that capacity grow?**

## What we found

Across five analytical lenses — management theory, managerial practice, enterprise operating models, competitive strategy, and economic decision-making — a consistent picture emerges:

1. **The bottleneck has migrated from execution to verification.** When agents can generate far more change than humans can review, the scarce resource is **verification bandwidth**: the amount of machine-generated change an organization can responsibly understand and validate. The planning question changes from "How much work can the team produce?" to "How much change can we afford to understand?"

2. **Scrum's empirical core survives; the labor-allocation practices around it do not.** Story points, velocity targets, task decomposition, and utilization planning were never part of Scrum's fundamentals — they were accretions from an era of scarce human execution. AI weakens those practices while strengthening what was always central: explicit goals, transparency, inspection, adaptation, and usable increments.

3. **The Sprint changes meaning rather than disappearing.** Machines can and should operate continuously; human sense-making remains periodic. The result is a dual-cadence organization — continuous machine execution below, timeboxed human learning above — in which the Sprint evolves from a production box into a learning cadence.

4. **Backlogs shift from work packages to intent packages.** When implementation is cheap, the valuable human input moves upstream: problem definition, constraints, acceptance scenarios, policy boundaries, and the evidence required before a solution can be trusted. The Definition of Done becomes a risk-based economic control — verification cost proportional to the cost of being wrong — rather than a uniform checklist.

5. **Activity metrics stop meaning anything.** Pull-request counts, lines of code, and even velocity become noise when agents generate output at near-zero marginal cost. Outcome, flow, reliability, and rework measures — such as the deployment rework rate in DORA's five-metric framework — become the signals that distinguish useful acceleration from accelerated churn.

6. **Competitive advantage moves to organizational capabilities, not tooling.** Companies that merely add agents to existing processes get local productivity gains; companies that redesign specifications, evaluation, risk classification, and decision rights around abundant execution move onto a different curve. The capital-allocation principle follows: fund the constraint, not the excitement — the next dollar belongs wherever verification, intent, or learning has become the binding bottleneck.
