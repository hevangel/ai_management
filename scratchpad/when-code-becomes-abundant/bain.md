# Stop Counting Code: How High-Performance Engineering Organizations Will Manage Talent in the AI Era

AI coding tools are creating a good problem for engineering leaders.

Developers can produce dramatically more software.

Unfortunately, many companies are still using management systems designed for a world in which producing software was expensive.

That mismatch matters.

When code becomes cheap, counting code tells you less.

When AI can generate pull requests, tests, documentation, and implementations in minutes, managers who continue rewarding visible activity risk encouraging exactly the wrong behavior: more software, more complexity, more review burden, and more downstream cost.

Leading organizations will make a different choice.

They will stop managing engineers around how much they produce and start managing them around how much value and difficulty they retire.

### The old scorecard is breaking

Most engineering leaders already know not to evaluate developers by lines of code.

In practice, however, substitutes often creep in:

- tickets closed;
- pull requests merged;
- commit activity;
- story points;
- cycle time;
- review volume.

These numbers feel objective.

AI makes them increasingly deceptive.

An employee with strong AI tools can generate enormous activity quickly. But more activity does not necessarily mean better outcomes.

It can mean:

- more code for reviewers to inspect;
- more vulnerabilities;
- more components to operate;
- more regressions;
- more systems future employees must maintain.

The fundamental management question is no longer:

**How much did this engineer produce?**

It is:

**Did this engineer make the business's engineering system better?**

### Winners will focus on five behaviors

High-performance organizations should anchor engineering reviews on five observable behaviors.

**1. Frame the right problem.** Strong engineers don't start by asking AI to generate code. They establish what outcome matters, what constraints apply, and what doesn't need to be built. AI makes this more — not less — important. A machine that can solve the wrong problem ten times faster simply allows the organization to waste money ten times faster.

**2. Retire uncertainty.** Complex work rarely progresses in a straight line. The engineer who proves that three proposed approaches will not work may have created more value than the engineer who rapidly implements a fourth without investigating the risks. Managers should look for evidence of uncertainty being removed: experiments, instrumentation, prototypes, decision logs, clearer requirements, resolved dependencies. Progress is not always something shipped. Sometimes progress is knowing what not to do next.

**3. Exercise judgment.** AI can generate options. Someone still has to choose. Strong engineers understand when the simplest solution is sufficient, when additional safeguards are justified, and when an apparently elegant design will create long-term operating cost. The value increasingly lies in the choice, not the generation.

**4. Own the outcome.** AI-assisted speed is worthless if the result creates problems elsewhere. Strong engineers verify their work. They think about tests, security, deployment, monitoring, rollback, documentation, and future maintenance. They don't throw AI-generated output over the wall and call themselves productive.

**5. Amplify the system.** The best engineers make other engineers better. AI expands this definition. High-leverage employees build reusable workflows, improve team knowledge, automate repetitive work, perform high-quality reviews, mentor colleagues, and learn how to orchestrate AI agents effectively. Their contribution shows up in the performance of the system around them.

### Don't replace bad metrics with AI metrics

There is an obvious temptation.

If commits no longer tell us enough, perhaps organizations should track AI usage instead.

Don't.

- Number of prompts is not performance.
- Number of tokens is not performance.
- Percentage of AI-generated code is not performance.
- AI suggestion acceptance is not performance.

Those are utilization metrics.

A developer who spends millions of tokens generating increasingly elaborate solutions may create less value than one who asks a few well-designed questions and discovers that the feature shouldn't be built.

Measure AI by the leverage it creates.

Did AI help the engineer investigate faster?

Did it improve validation?

Did it increase quality?

Did it allow the engineer to handle broader scope?

Did it make the team faster?

Those are business questions.

Token counts are not.

### Use data to ask better questions

Engineering organizations already have plenty of data.

Use it.

But use it correctly.

Pull-request history, CI results, incidents, reviews, tickets, tests, security findings, and deployment records can help managers understand what happened.

AI can automatically assemble that information into concise weekly or quarterly evidence packs.

That is a meaningful productivity improvement for managers.

But don't let the system make the final judgment.

A revert isn't automatically bad performance.

An engineer working on difficult infrastructure will naturally experience more failures than someone making low-risk UI changes.

A large PR isn't automatically poor practice.

A slow project isn't automatically an underperforming engineer.

**Metrics should tell managers where to look. They should not tell managers what to conclude.**

### Make performance problems easier to manage

The same approach makes underperformance easier to address.

Managers often get trapped asking:

Is this employee lazy?

Are they unlucky?

Are they working hard enough?

Those are poor management questions because effort and motivation are difficult to observe.

Instead, define the role outcomes clearly.

Then ask:

Is the employee framing problems appropriately?

Are they making measurable progress against uncertainty?

Are they escalating dependencies?

Are they verifying their work?

Are they delivering at the expected level?

Are they responding to feedback?

This creates a much cleaner improvement process.

AI can reduce administrative effort by assembling evidence and drafting summaries.

The manager remains accountable for the assessment.

Performance improvement should focus on observable expectations rather than inferred personality or effort.

### Reward the engineer who deletes the system

AI creates one more leadership challenge that companies shouldn't underestimate.

It makes building things dangerously easy.

Suppose one engineer generates 20,000 lines of new code and deploys three new services.

Another discovers that an existing platform already does most of what is required, modifies one interface, and deletes 8,000 lines of legacy code.

Who deserves greater recognition?

If the second solution produces the same or better business outcome, the answer should be obvious.

Yet many performance systems still contain a hidden bias toward creation.

That bias becomes costly when creation is nearly free.

Leading organizations will explicitly recognize:

- simplification;
- deletion;
- reuse;
- standardization;
- reduced dependencies;
- reduced operating burden.

The scarce skill will not be the ability to make AI produce more.

It will be knowing when enough is enough.

### Three actions for engineering leaders

Companies do not need to redesign their entire HR system overnight.

Start with three changes.

**Replace activity-based review conversations.** Structure reviews around problem framing, uncertainty reduction, judgment, responsible delivery, and leverage.

**Separate telemetry from evaluation.** Automate evidence collection aggressively. Automate employee judgment cautiously.

**Reward simplification and AI leverage.** Make it clear that using AI effectively means improving outcomes — not maximizing generated output.

These changes are relatively small.

Their effect is not.

### The bottom line

AI is changing the economics of software engineering.

Implementation is becoming abundant.

That means performance management must move toward what remains scarce:

judgment, context, accountability, risk management, coordination, and the ability to identify what actually deserves to be built.

Companies that continue counting the exhaust of engineering work will increasingly misidentify their best people.

Companies that measure the quality of decisions and outcomes will have a different advantage.

They won't simply get more code from AI.

They will get **better engineering from humans**.
