#### You said:

Had dinner with a tier 1 VC in NYC and we were chatting about Bending Spoons and he goes "The Bending Spoons guys are PE guys, they value businesses on EBITDA. In VC we value businesses on VIBEITDA, so its quite different"

I know EBITDA but what is vibeita?

#### ChatGPT said:

“VIBEITDA” is a joke, not an actual accounting metric. Your VC was making fun of how venture valuations work.

The punchline is:

**PE:** “What does this business actually earn?” → EBITDA → apply a multiple.

**VC:** “How exciting does this company _feel_?” → **VIBE-ITDA** → founder quality, growth rate, TAM, narrative, competitive buzz, brand, investor FOMO, whether it is in the hot sector of the moment, etc.

So imagine a completely fictional equation:

VIBEITDA=Growth×TAM×Founder charisma×Narrative×FOMO

And then:

Valuation≈20×–100×VIBEITDA

The joke is especially good in the context of **Bending Spoons**, because their model is almost the opposite of classic Silicon Valley VC thinking. They buy established software businesses, focus intensely on monetization, costs and cash generation, and are willing to cut headcount aggressively. In other words, they ask something much closer to:

> “How much cash can this asset produce after we optimize it?”

rather than:

> “Could this become a $100 billion category-defining platform?”

That distinction actually goes pretty deep.

A typical PE-style investor might see a software company doing:

**$100m revenue, $25m EBITDA**

and say:

EV=15×EBITDA=$375m

A VC might see another company doing:

**$100m revenue, –$30m EBITDA, growing 100%**

and say:

> “$2 billion seems reasonable.”

Because they're underwriting something like:

**$100m ARR → $200m → $400m → $800m**, with the assumption that margins can come later.

That imaginary future profitability is basically what your VC is jokingly calling **VIBEITDA**.

And there is another layer to the joke: **VC valuation is partially endogenous.**

If Sequoia invests at $2bn, suddenly other investors may conclude:

> “Sequoia must know something.”

Employees join because Sequoia invested. Customers take the company more seriously. More VCs want into the next round. The next round happens at $5bn.

So the “vibe” can temporarily become an economic asset in its own right.

PE can't usually play that game because eventually the PE investor needs:

Purchase Price→EBITDA growth→debt repayment→exit multiple→IRR

The cash flows have to show up.

VC instead has a much more convex payoff structure. If 15 investments die and one becomes OpenAI/Uber/Airbnb-scale, the fund can still work. So paying what looks like an insane price based on current earnings can actually be rational **if the right tail is sufficiently enormous**.

So I would translate your VC's sentence as:

> **“Bending Spoons prices companies based on cash flows. We price companies based on the probability that everyone will eventually believe they are enormously valuable.”**

He was exaggerating for comedic effect, but there is a surprisingly serious piece of finance underneath it. The web doesn't appear to show “VIBEITDA” as a recognized finance term; it looks like insider VC slang/wordplay rather than a standard metric. [![](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.linkedin.com&sz=128)LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/elyse-sholk-9088151b?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The really interesting question is **whether VC “vibes” can be converted into a rigorous valuation framework**. They actually can: mathematically, a lot of VIBEITDA is just **real-option value + power-law outcomes + probability-weighted future monopoly profits**. That explains why VC and PE can look at the _same company_ and rationally arrive at valuations 5–10× apart.

#### You said:

So.. VC is growing companies. Pe is stable companies that lack growth

#### ChatGPT said:

Broadly, yes — but the cleaner distinction is **uncertainty and cash flow**, not simply “growth vs no growth.”

VC usually invests in companies where the business is still being built: product-market fit may be evolving, revenue can be growing very fast, profits may be negative, and most of the value depends on what the company could become. The investor is underwriting future scale.

PE usually buys companies where the economics are already proven: meaningful revenue, predictable customers, established margins or a believable path to margins, and enough cash flow to support a valuation and often debt. The investor is underwriting what the business already is, plus operational improvements.

So a useful shorthand is:

- **VC:** create the machine.

- **PE:** optimize the machine.


But PE absolutely likes growth. In fact, a PE firm would much rather buy a company growing 15% with strong cash flow than one growing 0%. They just usually don't want to pay for highly speculative growth.

A classic PE value-creation equation is something like:

Return=EBITDA growth+margin expansion+debt paydown+multiple change

And EBITDA growth can come from organic growth, price increases, acquisitions, geographic expansion, new products, etc.

VC is more like:

Return≈probability of massive outcome×size of massive outcome

That's why VC can tolerate:

$50mrevenue,−$40mEBITDA

if it believes the company could eventually become a $10bn–$50bn business.

PE generally wants something more like:

$500mrevenue,$100mEBITDA

and asks:

> Can we turn $100m EBITDA into $180m over five years?

There's also an important middle category: **growth equity**. That's basically the bridge between VC and PE. The company is already established and often near profitability, but still growing 30–50%+, and the investor is mostly paying for growth rather than financial engineering.

So I'd think of the spectrum as:

**VC → Growth Equity → PE Buyout**

with the company gradually moving from **story + potential** toward **cash flow + predictability**.

And that makes the Bending Spoons comment more precise: they aren't necessarily buying “bad, no-growth companies.” They are buying businesses where **the existing economics can be measured and optimized**, which is much more PE-like than classic VC.

Yes, from an employee perspective, that is a pretty good rule of thumb — with some important caveats.

A company **just acquired by PE** is often the least attractive moment to join if you care about job security or upside. The first 12–24 months can involve headcount cuts, management changes, tighter budgets, KPI pressure, centralization, pricing changes, and aggressive efficiency targets. The whole point of the acquisition may be to increase EBITDA, so labor cost is an obvious lever.

After that optimization phase, though, the company can become a perfectly decent place to work: more disciplined, profitable, less chaotic, clearer processes, and often more stable. The tradeoff is that the upside is usually capped. Unless you are senior enough to receive meaningful management equity, your compensation is mostly salary + bonus. You are not typically sitting on stock that can 20x.

VC-backed companies are almost the mirror image. The company is usually trying to maximize enterprise value, not current profit. It may knowingly burn cash to grow faster. Employees often accept more chaos, lower certainty, and sometimes lower cash compensation in exchange for equity.

So the employee payoff looks roughly like this:

PE employee≈salary stability+modest bonus+limited equity upside

versus

VC employee≈salary+high failure risk+lottery-ticket equity

And yes, **“go big or go home” is much closer to the VC model**.

A VC-backed startup often cannot afford to become merely “a decent $100m company.” If investors put in capital at a $1–3bn valuation, a mediocre outcome can still be a disaster for the later investors and employees holding common stock.

The company may need to become:

$10B,$30B,$100B+

for the economics of the fund to really work.

That creates very different behavior.

PE asks:

> “How do we make this business reliably more profitable?”

VC asks:

> “How do we make this business vastly bigger?”

There is another subtle difference for employees: **VC upside is much more skewed than people think**.

Suppose you join a startup and receive stock options theoretically worth $300k at the current valuation. That does **not** mean you have a $300k asset. The eventual result might be:

$0

very frequently,

or perhaps:

$100k−$500k

in an ordinary successful exit,

or, rarely:

$5m+

if you joined an exceptional company early.

So VC employment is closer to owning a call option on the company.

PE employment is closer to owning a bond: lower variance, lower upside.

There is also one category where I would actually be quite interested in joining a newly PE-owned company: **if you're a senior executive brought in specifically for the transformation**.

Then you might get a management incentive plan where you own, say, a small percentage of the equity. If the PE fund buys the company for $2B and exits it at $5B five years later, that management equity can be very valuable.

In that case, you're economically participating like the PE fund itself.

But for an ordinary engineer, salesperson, analyst, designer, etc., your instinct is reasonable:

**Immediately after PE acquisition:** highest restructuring risk, limited upside.

**Mature PE-owned company after restructuring:** potentially comfortable and stable, but not much lottery-ticket upside.

**VC startup:** much more dangerous, but if you choose the right company early enough, substantially greater upside.

Which is why, if you're optimizing for wealth rather than comfort, the most interesting employment opportunity is often not simply “VC-backed.” It's **a VC-backed company that has already demonstrated product-market fit but whose valuation has not yet fully priced in the outcome**.

That's the employee version of finding a mispriced stock.

**Highest expected lottery-ticket upside:** great early VC startup.

## The interests of the company and employee are separating

This is the biggest conceptual update I'd make to our earlier framework.

Before AI, if your company grew enormously, employees generally benefited:

Company wins⇒employees mostly win

because the company needed more people, promoted people into management, paid retention packages, created whole new departments, etc.

AI can produce:

Company wins enormously+most employees capture little of the gain​

A company could double output while reducing headcount 20%.

That's fantastic shareholder productivity.

It isn't employee productivity in the traditional sense.

## So I would replace VC / public / PE with a new 2×2

For an employee, ask two questions:

|  | **My work is AI-leveraged** | **My work is AI-substitutable** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Company benefits from AI** | **Best place to be** | Company wins; you may lose |
| **Company threatened by AI** | Risky turnaround opportunity | **Worst place to be** |

That distinction is becoming more important than whether Blackstone, Sequoia or public shareholders own the company.

Consider two engineers at exactly the same public company.

Engineer A manages architecture, understands the customer, decomposes ambiguous problems and effectively directs 20 coding agents.

Engineer B receives clearly defined Jira tickets and turns specifications into implementation.

AI may make A dramatically more valuable while simultaneously undermining B's bargaining power.

They're sitting in the same company, with the same stock and the same ownership structure.

Their economic futures diverge.

## And that connects directly to our token-budget discussion

We were talking about the uncomfortable question:

Human salary↔token expenditure

and how nobody really knows the width of that substitution range yet.

That uncertainty is **the central management problem of the AI company**.

Pre-AI management allocated:

headcount

AI-native management increasingly allocates:

human judgment+agent capacity+tokens/compute​

Management might eventually ask:

> Should I give Alice another engineer costing $250k/year, or $100k/year of agent compute?

> Give me the compute.

> If Alice + agents can do what five engineers used to do, why do we have five?

### My revised employment hierarchy

I therefore wouldn't say anymore:

**VC > growing public > mature public > PE**

I'd say:

**#1 — Scarce human in an AI-native high-growth company with meaningful ownership.** This is the new jackpot. It could be VC-backed or public.

**#2 — Scarce human at a public company that is an AI winner, paid heavily in RSUs.** Probably still the best risk-adjusted wealth proposition.

**#3 — Ordinary employee at a healthy public company.** Much less safe than it used to look, because productivity gains can accrue primarily to shareholders.

**#4 — Ordinary employee at a VC startup.** The startup may succeed spectacularly without ever needing enough people for your particular skills to become scarce.

**#5 — Automatable employee in a PE transformation.** Probably the position I'd least want to occupy because PE has both the incentive and organizational machinery to turn AI productivity directly into EBITDA.

So the new dividing line isn't really:

> **VC = growth, PE = efficiency, public = stability.**

It's becoming:

> **Are you part of the scarce layer that directs AI capital, or are you part of the labor layer that AI capital is being deployed against?**

That, I think, is the AI-era replacement for the old worldview. [![](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.mckinsey.com&sz=128)McKinsey & Company+1](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai?os=av.&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### MIT Sloan Management Review version

Beyond Headcount: AI Is Changing the Unit of Organizational Capacity

# Beyond Headcount: AI Is Changing the Unit of Organizational Capacity

For most of modern management, headcount has served as a rough proxy for organizational capacity. A business unit with 500 employees was assumed to possess more productive capacity than one with 100. If demand increased substantially, managers generally expected employment to increase with it. Technology could make each worker more productive, but the organizational model remained fundamentally human-centered: managers allocated people, organized them into teams, and purchased technology to help those people work.

Generative AI and increasingly autonomous AI agents challenge that model.

The change is not simply that AI makes employees more productive. Companies have adopted productivity-enhancing technologies for centuries. The more consequential change is that organizations can now purchase a variable quantity of cognitive capacity that can both complement human workers and, for some tasks, substitute for them.

That creates a management problem for which conventional headcount planning is poorly suited.

A manager deciding how to expand a software organization may soon face a choice such as this:

Should the next $250,000 of annual spending fund another engineer, or substantially more AI inference, coding agents, and related infrastructure for the engineers already on the team?

The correct answer will vary by task, organization, and technology. More important, nobody yet knows the full shape of the substitution curve. But the existence of the question itself represents an important change in the economics of organizational design.

Companies increasingly need to manage not merely headcount, but **productive capacity composed of humans and artificial agents**.

## The old division between growth and efficiency

A useful way to see the change is through two historically different models of corporate value creation.

Venture capital traditionally finances growth. A successful VC-backed company is expected to discover a large market, acquire customers rapidly, build products, enter new geographies, and eventually become much larger than it was when the investment was made.

Private equity, particularly the buyout model, has traditionally focused more heavily on the economics of an established business: margins, cash generation, capital structure, pricing, operating efficiency, and ultimately EBITDA.

A venture capitalist once joked to me over dinner that private equity values businesses on EBITDA, while venture capital values them on “VIBEITDA.” The joke works because it captures a real distinction. PE tends to place more weight on measurable economics today; VC can place enormous value on what a company might become tomorrow.

Underlying this distinction was an assumption so ordinary that managers rarely needed to state it:

**Rapid growth normally requires substantially more people.**

A software company that expanded revenue tenfold would normally need many more engineers, salespeople, product managers, support staff, finance professionals, recruiters, and managers. Growth and organizational expansion were tightly connected.

That meant the VC model and the PE model often pulled firms in different directions.

The growth-oriented company reinvested aggressively and added capacity.

The efficiency-oriented company attempted to extract more output and profit from existing capacity.

AI weakens that trade-off.

A company that can increase output faster than it increases human labor can potentially pursue both objectives simultaneously:

**VC-style growth with PE-style operating leverage.**

That possibility is more consequential than simply automating a few jobs.

## AI is becoming a variable factor of production

Managers have traditionally divided resources into categories such as labor and capital.

Human labor has distinctive characteristics. Employees must be recruited, selected, onboarded, motivated, coordinated, compensated, promoted, and sometimes replaced. Adding a highly skilled employee can take months, and removing one carries organizational and human consequences.

Traditional computing capital behaves differently. An organization purchases servers, software, cloud services, or equipment to support the work performed by people and systems.

AI agents occupy an unusual position between these categories.

Like software, they can be acquired incrementally and scaled rapidly. But increasingly, they can perform activities that managers previously assigned to knowledge workers: writing code, analyzing documents, researching alternatives, creating presentations, answering customer questions, testing software, preparing financial analysis, or coordinating multistep processes.

The result is a new production function that managers may need to conceptualize as:

**Output = f(human judgment, traditional capital, AI capacity)**

The third term matters because it is elastic in a way that organizational labor has rarely been.

An employee might be given twice as much agent capacity next month without hiring anyone. An engineering group could increase its token consumption tenfold during a difficult development cycle and reduce it afterward. Different employees may also produce radically different returns from exactly the same quantity of AI resources.

This begins to make AI capacity resemble a management resource rather than merely an IT expense.

## Task productivity is not organizational productivity

The temptation is to take impressive demonstrations of AI performance and immediately translate them into headcount reductions. Current evidence suggests that this would be a mistake.

A 2026 NBER study by Mert Demirer, Leon Musolff, and Liyuan Yang examined more than 500,000 software developers and successive generations of AI coding tools. Autonomous coding agents increased coding activity, measured through commits, dramatically. But the productivity gain attenuated as the researchers moved up the production hierarchy: the effect was much smaller for projects and only about 30% at the level of actual software releases. The researchers estimate low substitutability between AI and human effort and describe the pattern as evidence of human “weak links” elsewhere in the production process.

This distinction is crucial.

Producing code is not the same as shipping a product.

Producing a market analysis is not the same as making a good investment decision.

Producing customer responses is not the same as retaining customers.

Producing documents is not the same as improving the organization.

AI can make one stage of a production system radically cheaper without increasing the throughput of the entire system proportionately.

The bottleneck simply moves.

In software, for example, code generation may become abundant while architecture, requirements, validation, security, integration, and business judgment become scarcer.

That means the key managerial question is not:

“How many people can this AI replace?”

It is:

**“Where does the bottleneck move when this task becomes cheap?”**

Organizations that answer the first question may cut costs. Organizations that answer the second may redesign themselves.

## The substitution boundary is still being discovered

Current economy-wide evidence also argues against assuming that AI has already become a direct substitute for large amounts of employment.

Research using the U.S. Census Bureau’s 2026 AI supplement found that most adopting firms still use AI predominantly for augmentation. Employment reductions associated directly with AI were uncommon. At the same time, deeper functional deployment and operational investment were more closely associated with declining employment than casual worker-level use.

A separate 2026 survey of nearly 750 corporate executives found positive productivity effects with substantial variation across sectors and firms, particularly in high-skill services and finance. Importantly, the gains appeared to reflect improvements in total factor productivity and innovation rather than simply companies substituting machines for labor.

These findings suggest that managers should resist two symmetrical errors.

The first is treating AI as merely another employee productivity tool, like a better spreadsheet or IDE.

The second is treating AI as a known replacement ratio — for example, assuming that one agent equals some fraction of one full-time employee.

We do not yet possess such a stable conversion rate.

Instead, there is likely a **substitution range**.

For some tasks, additional AI expenditure can replace human effort almost directly.

For others, AI primarily complements expert workers.

For still others, additional tokens produce very little business value because some non-AI bottleneck constrains output.

That range is likely to differ not only between occupations but between individual workers performing nominally identical jobs.

## The return to human judgment may become more unequal

This creates a less obvious organizational consequence.

Suppose two engineers each cost a company $250,000 annually.

One engineer is particularly effective at decomposing ambiguous problems, specifying requirements, evaluating alternatives, supervising multiple agents, recognizing failure, and integrating generated work into a complex production system.

The other is highly effective at implementing clearly specified tasks manually.

Before capable AI agents, their relative productivity might differ modestly.

Afterward, their productivity may diverge sharply.

The first employee can potentially combine judgment with a large quantity of artificial execution capacity. The second employee's strongest capability increasingly overlaps with what the agents provide.

The organization therefore stops asking only:

“How productive is this person?”

It begins asking:

**“How much scalable AI capacity can this person's judgment productively direct?”**

This is analogous to an old phenomenon in management: talented executives create more value when they control more capital and larger organizations.

AI may extend that principle much further down the organizational hierarchy.

Some individual contributors may effectively become managers of artificial capacity without managing any humans.

The traditional distinction between “manager” and “individual contributor” consequently becomes less useful.

## Why flat AI budgets will fail

Many companies initially manage generative AI through familiar IT mechanisms. They buy enterprise licenses, set per-user limits, negotiate contracts, and allocate roughly equal access across employees.

That is sensible during experimentation. It is unlikely to remain economically rational.

Organizations do not give every manager the same number of employees merely in the interest of fairness. They allocate headcount according to opportunity, expected return, organizational need, and the manager's demonstrated ability to use resources effectively.

If AI agents become meaningful productive capacity, allocating the same token budget to every employee will eventually look equally arbitrary.

A developer who can convert $50,000 of annual agent usage into $500,000 of incremental organizational value should not necessarily receive the same allocation as someone who creates little incremental value after consuming $5,000.

Nor should management automatically constrain the first employee because the second employee has not used an equivalent amount.

AI therefore creates a capital-allocation problem inside the organization.

The scarce resource is no longer simply access to the model. It is the company's budget for artificial cognition.

## From headcount planning to capacity planning

Organizations will need a different management discipline.

Rather than beginning the annual planning cycle with the question “How many people does this group need?”, leaders should increasingly ask:

**“What combination of human judgment and artificial capacity produces the required business outcome at the best risk-adjusted cost?”**

That requires measuring three different relationships.

First, managers need to identify **complementarity**: where additional AI makes a particular employee or team disproportionately more capable.

Second, they need to identify **substitution**: where AI genuinely reduces the amount of human labor required for a stable level of output.

Third, they need to identify **bottlenecks**: where more AI output simply creates additional work elsewhere in the system.

This is fundamentally different from maximizing token utilization.

High utilization may even be undesirable. A team that consumes enormous quantities of inference while producing little customer or business value is no better than a department that hires aggressively without improving results.

The unit of analysis must therefore move upward from tokens, prompts, or tasks toward outcomes.

## A new kind of span of control

Management theory has long studied span of control: how many people one manager can effectively supervise.

AI introduces a related concept:

**span of agency — the amount of artificial execution capacity one human can effectively direct.**

This capacity is unlikely to be unlimited.

As agents generate more code, analysis, documents, experiments, or decisions, humans must still specify objectives, resolve ambiguity, verify critical work, and accept accountability.

At some point, adding another agent may create more supervisory overhead than additional output.

That point will differ radically among workers and tasks.

The most valuable employees in some AI-native organizations may therefore be distinguished less by how much work they personally execute and more by the productive system they can orchestrate.

This suggests a future in which a small team of highly capable humans manages an enormous quantity of machine-generated activity.

The firm grows without its organization growing proportionately.

## What happens to employees when the firm wins?

This possibility also changes an old assumption about the relationship between corporate success and employees.

Historically, rapidly growing companies created large quantities of organizational opportunity. Growth required hiring. Hiring created management layers. New products created departments. Geographic expansion created leadership positions.

Company growth therefore generated career growth for many employees even when they owned little equity.

AI weakens that mechanism.

A company could conceivably double output and increase enterprise value while keeping employment flat — or even reducing it.

That produces a new distributional possibility:

**The firm becomes dramatically more productive without the median employee capturing a proportionate share of the productivity gain.**

The effect will depend heavily on ownership and compensation.

At a venture-backed company, a small group of employees with meaningful equity may capture extraordinary upside if a very small team creates enormous enterprise value.

At a public company, employees with substantial stock compensation may participate directly in rising margins and valuation.

In a private-equity-owned business, management may convert AI-driven productivity primarily into EBITDA, with the gains flowing mostly toward owners and members of management who participate in equity incentive plans.

The old categories of VC, public company, and PE therefore remain relevant — but for a different reason.

The critical question is increasingly not simply what type of company someone works for.

It is:

**Who owns the productivity gain?**

## The AI-native firm may combine what used to be opposing models

For decades, companies often chose between maximizing growth and maximizing efficiency.

Venture capital favored the former.

Private equity became particularly skilled at the latter.

AI has the potential to weaken this distinction because growth no longer necessarily requires proportional additions of human labor.

A company that can grow revenue much faster than headcount can generate both rapid expansion and increasing operating leverage.

In financial terms, this is extremely attractive.

In organizational terms, it is disruptive.

The company may no longer resemble the managerial corporation that dominated the twentieth century — a pyramid containing thousands of human specialists coordinated through multiple layers of management.

Instead, an AI-native firm may resemble a much smaller network of people controlling large amounts of elastic artificial capacity.

The challenge for today's executives is that we do not yet know where the equilibrium lies.

AI is clearly capable of substituting for some cognitive tasks. It is equally clearly complementary to human judgment in many production systems. Current research suggests substantial productivity gains but also strong bottlenecks and limited evidence, so far, of economy-wide labor replacement.

Managers therefore should not begin with a predetermined target for reducing headcount.

They should begin by discovering their organization's **human-AI production function**.

The central management problem of the next decade may be learning where human salaries and AI expenditures are interchangeable, where they are complementary, and where neither matters because some other constraint dominates.

When that is understood, the familiar annual question —

**“How much headcount do we need?”**

— will sound increasingly incomplete.

The more useful question will be:

**“How much productive capacity do we need, and what is the optimal combination of humans and machines to create it?”**

Headcount built the vocabulary of the pre-AI corporation.

Capacity may become the vocabulary of the next one.

The Sloan draft deliberately stops short of pretending we already know a stable human-to-AI replacement ratio; that restraint is consistent with the strongest current empirical evidence. ### Harvard Business Review version

Stop Planning Headcount. Start Planning Human-AI Capacity.

# Stop Planning Headcount. Start Planning Human-AI Capacity.

At dinner recently, a venture capitalist joked that private equity values companies on EBITDA, while venture capital values them on “VIBEITDA.”

Behind the joke lies an old distinction in corporate strategy.

Venture capital funds companies to grow. Private equity buys businesses and makes them more efficient. The VC-backed company adds products, customers, markets — and usually people. The PE-backed company focuses more heavily on margins, productivity, and cash flow.

AI is beginning to break that distinction.

For most of business history, significant growth required significant additions of labor. A software company that increased revenue tenfold normally needed many more engineers, salespeople, customer-support representatives, product managers, and managers.

AI makes another possibility increasingly plausible:

**A company can get much bigger without becoming proportionately bigger.**

That is good news for shareholders. It presents managers with a problem for which most organizations are poorly prepared.

Their budgeting systems still separate headcount from technology.

Managers request people. IT purchases software. Finance controls both budgets.

But increasingly, those resources are partially interchangeable.

A software leader may soon face a very practical question:

**Should I spend the next $250,000 on another engineer — or give my existing engineers $250,000 worth of additional AI-agent capacity?**

No general formula can answer that question today.

But every leadership team needs a process for answering it inside its own organization.

## AI is no longer just a software expense

When companies first adopted generative AI, giving employees access looked much like deploying any other productivity application.

Buy licenses. Establish security policies. Train people. Measure adoption.

That framing is becoming obsolete.

An AI coding agent does not merely help an engineer type faster. It can increasingly receive a task, inspect a code base, write code, execute tests, diagnose failures, and revise its own work.

An AI research agent can search, analyze, synthesize, and prepare a first-pass recommendation.

A customer-service agent can handle portions of a workflow that previously required human representatives.

Once a technology performs work rather than simply supporting work, its budget begins to overlap economically with the labor budget.

That does **not** mean AI and employees are interchangeable one-for-one.

Current evidence suggests something more complicated — and more important.

A 2026 study of more than 500,000 software developers found that autonomous coding agents increased coding activity dramatically. But the apparent gain shrank sharply as researchers moved from code commits to completed software releases. At the release level, the effect was roughly 30%, suggesting that human and organizational bottlenecks constrained how much task-level productivity reached the customer.

That finding should change how executives think about AI.

The question is not simply, “How much labor can we automate?”

It is:

**“If AI makes this task nearly free, what becomes the next constraint?”**

## The bottleneck moves

Consider a software organization.

If writing code becomes five times faster, software delivery does not necessarily become five times faster.

Requirements may become the constraint.

Or architecture.

Or testing.

Or security review.

Or customer validation.

Or simply management's ability to decide what should be built.

Adding more AI capacity after that point may generate more code while delivering almost no additional business value.

This is why an executive mandate such as “every team must reduce headcount by 20% because of AI” is premature.

It is equally misguided to say, “AI will only augment our people; we will never reduce headcount.”

Both positions assume the answer before measuring the production system.

Recent U.S. firm-level evidence illustrates the uncertainty. Most companies using AI still report augmentation rather than job elimination, and direct employment reductions remain uncommon. But deeper operational deployment appears more associated with employment declines than casual employee-level AI usage.

Management therefore needs to discover the substitution boundary experimentally.

## Replace headcount planning with capacity planning

Most organizations begin resource planning with positions.

“We need six more engineers.”

“We need three analysts.”

“We need 20 customer-service representatives.”

Instead, leaders should increasingly require teams to define the business capacity they need before specifying whether it should come from humans or AI.

A useful sequence is:

**Outcome → bottleneck → human judgment required → AI capacity required → headcount required**

That reverses the traditional process.

Suppose a product organization wants to double the number of experiments it can run each quarter.

The old response might be to add engineers and analysts.

The new response should first determine where experiment throughput is constrained.

If coding is the bottleneck, AI agents may provide the cheapest additional capacity.

If product judgment is the bottleneck, the company may need a senior product leader.

If customer feedback is the bottleneck, neither more engineers nor more tokens solves the problem.

The resource follows the constraint.

This sounds obvious, but few headcount processes work this way. Headcount remains an organizational currency. Managers negotiate for positions because positions confer capacity, status, resilience, and future managerial scope.

AI makes that currency less reliable.

## Don't give everyone the same AI budget

Many organizations are starting AI deployment by giving every employee roughly equal access to models or agents.

That is a sensible way to encourage experimentation.

It is a poor long-term capital-allocation strategy.

Imagine two engineers.

Both cost $250,000 a year.

Engineer A can use $50,000 of AI resources to dramatically increase the amount of valuable work a team ships.

Engineer B sees little additional improvement after $5,000 of AI usage.

Should both receive the same annual AI allowance?

Probably not.

Organizations don't give every manager an identical headcount budget. A manager who can profitably deploy 100 people may be given a much larger organization than a manager whose opportunity requires 10.

AI capacity should eventually be allocated the same way: according to marginal return.

This creates a new managerial discipline.

Finance departments will need to treat some AI spending less like software licensing and more like **deployable productive capacity**.

## Measure output, not token consumption

Once managers begin allocating AI capacity, they will be tempted to measure utilization.

How many prompts did employees send?

How many agents did they run?

How many tokens did a team consume?

Those metrics may be useful operationally, but they are dangerous management targets.

A team can consume twice as many tokens without creating twice as much value.

Indeed, abundant AI can produce a new form of organizational waste: more code nobody needs, more analysis nobody acts on, more documents nobody reads, and more machine-generated work that humans must review.

The correct denominator therefore moves upward.

For software teams, it might be reliable customer-facing releases.

For sales teams, qualified pipeline or revenue.

For finance, decision accuracy and cycle time.

For support, resolution quality and customer retention.

The objective is not to maximize AI use.

It is to maximize valuable output per dollar of combined human and AI capacity.

## Identify your AI multipliers

AI will not increase every employee's value equally.

That may become one of its most consequential effects.

An employee who is good at executing well-specified tasks may benefit from AI, but their capabilities may also overlap significantly with what agents can perform.

Another employee may be unusually good at deciding what problem to solve, decomposing ambiguous work, specifying objectives, recognizing bad output, integrating multiple domains of knowledge, and accepting responsibility for the result.

Give that person additional AI capacity and something different happens.

Their judgment becomes leveraged.

One highly capable employee may increasingly direct the equivalent of a small virtual team.

The important organizational metric may therefore become not just individual productivity, but what we might call an employee's **span of agency**:

**How much artificial execution capacity can this person productively direct?**

Managers have long recognized that executives differ in the amount of human organization and financial capital they can effectively manage.

AI may bring the same phenomenon to individual contributors.

The best engineer may increasingly resemble the manager of a machine workforce even if no human reports to them.

Companies should identify these people early.

They may become disproportionately valuable.

## Don't assume productivity gains belong to employees

There is another implication leaders need to confront.

In the pre-AI corporation, company growth usually created employee opportunity.

A company that doubled revenue often hired aggressively. New teams created management jobs. New products created departments. Geographic expansion created new leadership positions.

Employees could benefit from growth even without owning much stock because growth itself created career paths.

AI potentially breaks that link.

A company may double revenue while barely increasing headcount.

It could even grow while reducing employment.

That outcome may be economically excellent for shareholders. But it means managers can no longer assume that corporate productivity and employee prosperity move together automatically.

Who captures the AI productivity gain will increasingly depend on ownership and compensation.

Founders and early employees with substantial equity may do extraordinarily well in a small, highly leveraged AI-native company.

Public-company employees receiving significant stock compensation may participate in higher margins and share-price appreciation.

Employees paid primarily through salary may see much less of the gain.

This will make compensation design an increasingly important part of AI transformation.

If a company asks a small number of employees to manage dramatically more productive capacity, those employees will eventually ask why their compensation should still reflect a pre-AI definition of one job.

## Four changes leaders should make now

The technology is changing too quickly for companies to determine a permanent human-to-AI exchange rate. They don't need one.

They need a management system capable of learning it.

First, **budget for outcomes before positions**. Require teams to define the capacity or business result they need before approving additional headcount.

Second, **pool some AI spending with labor spending**. Managers should be able, within appropriate controls, to choose between additional human capacity and additional artificial capacity rather than treating them as completely unrelated budgets.

Third, **measure the bottleneck after every major productivity gain**. When AI makes one stage of work much cheaper, redirect management attention toward whatever now limits throughput.

Fourth, **allocate AI unequally when the economics justify it**. Employees and teams that demonstrate a high return from additional agent capacity should be allowed to consume more of it.

This does not mean reducing every organization to a spreadsheet in which salaries and tokens are perfectly interchangeable.

They aren't.

Human beings possess judgment, accountability, organizational knowledge, relationships, creativity, and moral responsibility that cannot simply be expressed as an inference bill.

But neither is it credible anymore to pretend that the two budgets are entirely unrelated.

## The new management question

For decades, managers have asked a familiar question during planning season:

**How much headcount do we need?**

That question embeds an assumption that organizational capacity is principally a function of how many people the company employs.

AI is making the assumption increasingly inaccurate.

The next generation of management will have to answer a harder question:

**What combination of human judgment and artificial capacity should we deploy against this opportunity?**

We don't yet know exactly how wide the range of substitution between those resources will become.

That uncertainty is not a reason to postpone the question.

It is the reason companies need to begin measuring it now.

The companies that learn the answer fastest may discover something remarkable: they no longer need to choose between the growth economics traditionally associated with venture capital and the operating leverage traditionally associated with private equity.

They may be able to achieve both.

And when they do, headcount will no longer tell us how large an organization really is.

When the source is a conversation, distinguish between:

- early exploratory ideas,

- ideas the author later rejected,

- ideas the author refined,

- and the final position.


Use the most developed version of the argument.

* * *

# Stage 1: Extract the Intellectual Core

Before generating the five articles, create an internal source model containing:

## Central Thesis

Express the strongest version of the argument in one or two sentences.

## Conventional View

Explain the established assumption, management practice, or worldview being challenged.

## New Observation

Identify what has changed.

## Causal Mechanism

Explain why the change matters rather than merely describing it.

Use a simple causal chain when appropriate:

A → B → C → managerial consequence.

## Key Tension

Identify the unresolved trade-off, contradiction, or uncertainty.

## Evidence

Separate:

- empirical evidence,

- research findings,

- company examples,

- personal observations,

- logical arguments,

- analogies,

- speculative implications.


## Useful Concepts

Extract any original terminology, equations, frameworks, metaphors, or memorable phrases from the source.

Preserve particularly strong phrases when appropriate.

## Managerial Implications

Identify what executives, managers, investors, or employees would need to do differently if the thesis is correct.

* * *

# Stage 2: Research and Verification

When the article depends on current facts, companies, AI capabilities, market conditions, economic data, or recent research, verify them before writing.

Prefer:

- original research papers,

- NBER,

- academic journals,

- government statistics,

- company filings,

- authoritative industry research,

- major consulting research when primary evidence is unavailable.


Do not use a consulting firm's claim as proof merely because the article is being written in consulting style.

Distinguish clearly between:

- what current evidence demonstrates,

- what evidence suggests,

- and what is a forward-looking hypothesis.


Avoid fake precision.

If no reliable human-to-AI substitution ratio exists, say so rather than inventing one.

* * *

# Stage 3: Generate Five Independent Articles

Each article should feel as if a different editorial team independently developed the same underlying insight.

Do not preserve identical section structure across all five.

Do not simply change headings.

* * *

# 1\. MIT Sloan Management Review Style

## Editorial Question

**What new model or management concept is needed to understand what is changing?**

## Character

Analytical, research-oriented, conceptually rigorous, but accessible to senior managers.

The article should feel halfway between an academic paper and a management magazine article.

## Structure

Typically:

1. Introduce an observed phenomenon or contradiction.

2. Explain why the existing management model is insufficient.

3. Develop a new conceptual framework.

4. Support it with evidence or research.

5. Explore second-order organizational consequences.

6. Discuss limitations and uncertainties.

7. Translate the model into management implications.

8. End with a broader conceptual shift.


## Style

Allow equations, conceptual models, and new terminology when they clarify the argument.

Examples:

Output = f(Human judgment, AI capacity, other constraints)

or:

Traditional unit of capacity → Headcount

Emerging unit of capacity → Human + machine productive capacity

Avoid oversimplifying uncertainty.

## Preferred Outcome

The reader should finish thinking:

**“I now have a new way of understanding the organization.”**

* * *

# 2\. Harvard Business Review Style

## Editorial Question

**What management practice is becoming wrong, and what should leaders do instead?**

## Character

Direct, polished, executive-friendly, actionable.

Move quickly from observation to management consequence.

## Structure

Typically:

1. Open with a recognizable management problem or anecdote.

2. State why conventional practice is becoming inadequate.

3. Explain the new reality.

4. Present an actionable framework.

5. Give concrete management examples.

6. Provide three to five actions leaders can implement.

7. Close with the new question leaders should ask.


## Style

Use clear managerial language.

Prefer:

“Stop doing X. Start doing Y.”

or:

“Leaders should make four changes.”

Do not spend too long developing abstract theory before reaching action.

## Preferred Outcome

The reader should finish thinking:

**“I need to change how I manage this.”**

* * *

# 3\. McKinsey-Style Insight Article

## Editorial Question

**What operating model should the enterprise build to respond to this change?**

## Character

Structured, enterprise-wide, transformation-oriented.

Translate the insight into an organizational architecture.

## Structure

Usually:

1. Define the structural shift.

2. Explain why the existing operating model is insufficient.

3. Introduce a future-state operating model.

4. Break it into 4–6 dimensions or shifts.

5. Show movement from current state to future state.

6. Discuss implications across functions.

7. Present a transformation path or maturity model.

8. Close with the capabilities required to make the transition.


## Framework Preference

Use transformations such as:

From → To

Examples:

Headcount planning → Capacity planning

Equal AI access → ROI-based allocation

Task automation → Workflow redesign

Span of control → Span of agency

Usage metrics → Business-outcome metrics

Include an exhibit-style table when useful.

## Preferred Outcome

The reader should finish thinking:

**“I can see what the future operating model looks like.”**

* * *

# 4\. BCG-Style Strategy Article

## Editorial Question

**How does this change the basis of competition, and what separates winners from losers?**

## Character

Strategic, competitive, provocative, archetype-driven.

Focus on structural advantage rather than internal process alone.

## Structure

Typically:

1. Identify a changing basis of competition.

2. Contrast the old economic model with the emerging one.

3. Show why incremental adoption is insufficient.

4. Define 3–5 strategic archetypes.

5. Explain which archetype has structural advantage.

6. Identify the economic mechanism behind that advantage.

7. Explain what incumbents risk if they move too slowly.

8. End with the strategic question CEOs should be asking.


## Framework Preference

Use strong contrasts.

Examples:

AI-augmented incumbent

vs.

AI-native scaler

or:

Headcount-led growth

vs.

Capacity-led growth

Focus on cost curves, competitive moats, scaling economics, and asymmetry.

## Preferred Outcome

The reader should finish thinking:

**“This could fundamentally change who wins in this industry.”**

* * *

# 5\. Bain-Style Value-Creation Article

## Editorial Question

**Where is the economic value, and how should capital be allocated to capture it?**

## Character

Economically grounded, execution-oriented, ROI-driven.

Reduce abstract claims to measurable business decisions.

## Structure

Typically:

1. Start with a concrete investment or operating decision.

2. Identify why current budgeting creates poor allocation.

3. Introduce a measurable economic framework.

4. Separate sources of value.

5. Explain diminishing returns and bottlenecks.

6. Connect operational metrics to P&L outcomes.

7. Provide decision rules.

8. End with a capital-allocation question.


## Framework Preference

Whenever appropriate, compare marginal returns.

For example:

ROI\_human =

Incremental business value from additional human capacity /

Fully loaded human cost

ROI\_AI =

Incremental business value from additional AI capacity /

AI + infrastructure + oversight cost

ROI\_hybrid =

Incremental business value from combined human-AI capacity /

Combined incremental cost

Consider three economic zones:

1. Complementarity

2. Substitution

3. Saturation


## Preferred Outcome

The reader should finish thinking:

**“I know what to measure and where I should put the next dollar.”**

# Important Differentiation Rules

The five articles must not have identical theses at identical levels of abstraction.

Translate the common insight into five different questions:

Sloan:

**What new theory explains this?**

HBR:

**What should managers change?**

McKinsey:

**What operating model should companies build?**

BCG:

**What new source of competitive advantage emerges?**

Bain:

**How should companies allocate resources to capture the value?**

The central insight may remain consistent, but each article should develop a different implication of it.

* * *

# Tone Rules

Write for intelligent senior business readers.

Avoid:

- generic AI enthusiasm,

- excessive buzzwords,

- fake certainty,

- simplistic “AI will replace everyone” arguments,

- repeated phrases such as “in today's rapidly changing world,”

- consulting clichés without analytical content,

- invented quantitative claims.


Prefer:

- strong causal reasoning,

- explicit assumptions,

- counterarguments,

- concrete examples,

- economic mechanisms,

- memorable but defensible frameworks.


* * *

# Handling Raw Conversations

Conversation transcripts often contain valuable ideas mixed with repetition and exploratory thinking.

Do not write the transcript chronologically.

Instead:

1. Reconstruct the argument.

2. Remove dead ends.

3. Combine repeated observations.

4. Preserve genuinely insightful turns in reasoning.

5. Identify where one idea led to another.

6. Convert conversational questions into explicit hypotheses.


For example:

Raw conversation:

“VC is growth. PE is efficiency.”

then later:

“But AI lets companies grow without hiring proportionally.”

then later:

“So maybe VC economics and PE economics merge.”

Should become:

Traditional corporate finance treated growth and operating leverage as partially opposing objectives. AI may weaken that trade-off by allowing output to scale faster than human labor, creating the possibility of VC-style growth combined with PE-style operating leverage.

* * *

# Handling Existing Articles

When the source is already a polished article:

Do not merely summarize and rephrase it.

Instead:

1. Extract the intellectual content.

2. Identify what is essential and what belongs only to the original format.

3. Reconstruct the argument independently for each destination style.

4. Preserve facts and evidence.

5. Change framing, hierarchy, examples, and managerial implications as required.

# Final Quality Check

Before delivering, verify that:

- all five articles share the same underlying source insight;

- none is merely a synonym-swapped copy;

- Sloan contains the strongest conceptual model;

- HBR contains the clearest managerial actions;

- McKinsey contains the clearest operating model;

- BCG contains the strongest competitive strategy argument;

- Bain contains the strongest economic decision framework;

- factual claims are supported;

- speculation is identified as speculation;

- no personal experience has been invented;

- the author's strongest original ideas have not been diluted.
